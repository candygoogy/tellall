The fall of the Western Roman Empire was not the result of a single catastrophe, but rather a slow collapse driven by severe internal decay and relentless external pressures. Politically, chronic instability, rampant corruption, and frequent civil wars eroded central authority, leaving the imperial government ineffective. Economically, crippling taxation, rampant inflation, and a heavy reliance on slave labor weakened domestic production and drained public treasuries. These domestic crises crippled the military, forcing Rome to rely on Germanic mercenaries whose loyalty to the state was tenuous at best. Simultaneously, the Migration Period pushed displaced Germanic tribes, including the Visigoths and Vandals, across vulnerable imperial borders. Overwhelmed by outside invasions and hollowed out from within, Rome could no longer defend its vast frontiers. When Germanic chieftain Odoacer deposed Emperor Romulus Augustulus in 476 CE, the Western Roman Empire finally fractured, permanently transforming the political landscape of Western Europe.
